# 11.20. Harness Benchmarking — Controlled Cross-Harness Evaluation

> Companion to [11.14. Tuning the Harness, Not the Model](./11.14.%20Tuning%20the%20Harness%2C%20Not%20the%20Model%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Harness%20as%20an%20Optimization%20Surface.md) and [11.18. Tool Architecture — The Interface as a Behavioral Knob](./11.18.%20Tool%20Architecture%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Interface%20as%20a%20Behavioral%20Knob.md). 11.14 argues the harness is worth optimizing. 11.18 measures one surface inside it. This note is the first published attempt to measure *whole harnesses against each other* under controlled conditions — and the headline is that the axis they separate on is cost, not quality.
>
> Methodology overlaps with [6. Evaluation & Observability](../6.%20Evaluation%20%26%20Observability/README.md); the eval design lessons here transfer to any agent benchmark you run yourself.

Every agent benchmark published so far varies the model and holds the scaffold fixed, or varies neither and reports a product. Runta's **FrontierHarness Eval** (2026-09-01) inverts it: one model, one task set, one runtime, twelve harness configurations. The result is a 17-point spread in pass rate and a **17.5× spread in cost per pass** attributable to the harness alone.

The number that should change how you budget is not the pass-rate spread. It is that the best and worst harnesses land within a few points of each other on quality while differing by an order of magnitude on spend — meaning harness choice is primarily a **cost decision that most teams currently make on vibes**.

---

## 1. What the design holds fixed

A cross-harness comparison is only readable if everything except the harness is pinned. The controls are the interesting part of the artifact:

| Variable | How it was pinned | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Model** | Kimi K3, served through Fireworks | A neutral model no harness ships as its native default — otherwise the vendor whose model you picked wins by construction |
| **API surface** | One gateway exposing both OpenAI- and Anthropic-shaped endpoints | Harnesses speak different protocols; without a shared gateway you are also measuring provider infrastructure |
| **Machine state** | Identical vCPU, memory, disk size, disk contents, memory state | Prevents a harness from benefiting from a warmer or fatter box |
| **Start condition** | All 360 trials restore from the same fresh checkpoint | Kills cross-run cache warmth, the most common silent bias in agent benchmarks |
| **Scoring** | Deterministic verifier-based pass/fail | No LLM judge, so no judge drift between configurations |
| **Cost accounting** | First-turn cache reads repriced consistently across harnesses | Different harnesses hit the cache differently on turn one; without normalization you compare billing quirks |

Scope: 30 tasks (21 Terminal-Bench, 9 DeepSWE), 9 harnesses across 12 configurations, 360 trials.

> **Why it matters:** the golden-checkpoint restore and the neutral-model choice are the two controls most in-house harness comparisons skip. Skip either and your "our harness is better" result is unfalsifiable.

Reported metric is `effective_cost_per_pass` — total cost across all tasks divided by the number of passes.

## 3. Quality clusters; cost does not

Pass rates occupy a 17-point band from 50.0% to 66.7%, and nine of the twelve configurations sit between 53% and 64%. On a 30-task suite that band is thin enough that most adjacent pairs are not separable.

Cost has no such structure. Two configurations tie exactly on pass rate — DSH Creator and Claude Code, both 63.3% — and differ **5.6×** on cost per pass. Across the field the extremes differ 17.5×.

> **Architectural takeaway:** treat pass rate as a gate and cost per pass as the objective. Any harness that clears your quality bar is a candidate; among candidates, the decision is purely economic. Teams that pick a harness on a pass-rate leaderboard are optimizing the variable with the least signal.

## 4. The cache-coupling trap

The Claude Code result is the single most instructive row, and it is **not** a quality finding about that harness. It is a portability finding.

Claude Code was built around Anthropic's explicit cache-breakpoint model, where the client declares where the prefix ends. Kimi K3 uses an implicit cache. The declared breakpoints do nothing, and the harness's context-shaping strategy — built on the assumption that it controls cache boundaries — becomes counterproductive. Runta reports its **token-weighted** cache hit rate at 25.0% against a field of 91.6–99.1%. (The plain cache hit rate in the results table, 67.8%, is a different base; the token-weighted figure is the one that tracks cost, because it weights by the tokens actually re-read.)

Cached reads cost roughly a tenth of fresh ones. A harness that loses the cache pays near-full price on every turn of a long trajectory, and long trajectories are the whole job.

> **Lesson:** a harness is **co-designed with a caching model**, not just with an API shape. Swapping the model underneath a harness silently invalidates its context strategy, and the failure shows up on the invoice rather than in the transcript. Before you run harness *H* on model *M*, check that *H*'s cache assumptions hold for *M* — and instrument token-weighted cache hit rate as a first-class harness metric. See [11.8. Context Engineering in the Harness](./11.8.%20Context%20Engineering%20in%20the%20Harness.md) on the append-never-mutate invariant this depends on.

This generalizes past this benchmark. Every "bring your own model" harness carries the same latent coupling.

## 5. Step efficiency is where the money actually goes

On one task, Pi reached the answer in **90 turns for $2.50**; Claude Code reached the same answer in **381 turns for $64.36**. Same model, same verifier, same outcome — 4.2× the turns and 26× the spend.

The multiplier compounds because agent cost is superlinear in turns: each additional turn re-sends a longer context. Turn count and cache hit rate are therefore not two independent problems. A harness that loops also pays the loop at rising prices.

```text
cost ≈ turns × context_length(turn) × (1 - cache_hit_rate) × price_fresh
                    ↑ grows with turns        ↑ harness-controlled
```

> **Why it matters:** "tokens per task" is the metric most teams log. It is downstream of two harness decisions you can actually change — how many turns the loop takes, and how much of each turn is a cache read. Log those two.

## 6. The preset is as consequential as the vendor

DeepSeek Harness shipped four presets in the same run: Creator (63.3% / $3.28), PTC (60.0% / $4.58), Standard (60.0% / $3.46), Minimal (56.7% / $4.72). Across them, **6.6 pass-rate points and roughly 1.4× cost, on identical infrastructure and identical code**.

The spread between DSH's best and worst preset is comparable to the spread between several distinct vendors. Configuration is not a footnote under the harness choice; it is a peer of it.

> **Architectural takeaway:** "we use harness X" is an underspecified statement about your system. Version and pin the preset the way you version the harness, and re-run your eval when it changes — this is the drift problem from [11.11. Operational Concerns](./11.11.%20Operational%20Concerns.md) applied to configuration rather than to rules.

## 7. Early stopping is a cost strategy, not a bug

Exo Harness capped at 51 steps. It posted the lowest pass rate in the field, 53.3%, and by a wide margin the lowest cost per pass, $1.05 — it failed cheaply where others succeeded expensively.

Whether that is the right trade depends entirely on what a failure costs you:

| If a failed run is… | Then a step cap is… |
|---|---|
| Cheap to retry, and retries are near-independent (a queued background task) | A strong lever — three cheap attempts can beat one expensive one |
| Expensive to detect, or the failure ships (an unsupervised write path) | A liability — you have bought cost savings with a higher escape rate |
| Blocking a human who is waiting | Ambiguous — the human's time may dominate both numbers |

Most harnesses have no explicit budget at all; they run until the model stops. A step or token cap is one of the few cost knobs available without touching the model, and it belongs in the same config layer as the permission rules.

## 8. What this does not establish

The benchmark's limitations are load-bearing, and reading past them is how you get burned:

- **Software engineering and terminal tasks only.** v1.0 makes no claim about knowledge work, research, browsing, or long-horizon multi-day agents. Whether the cost ranking survives a different task class is unmeasured.
- **Harnesses were tested exactly as shipped.** Nothing inside was modified, so a configuration's result reflects the whole bundle. You cannot attribute a win to any single component — this is the opposite of 11.18's isolate-one-variable design, and answers a different question.
- **One model.** Every cross-harness number is entangled with Kimi K3's cache semantics and tool-calling behavior. §4 is the proof that this entanglement is large, which means the ranking should be assumed **model-specific until re-run**.
- **30 tasks, 360 trials.** Roughly 30 trials per configuration. Adjacent rows are not separated by anything you should treat as significant.

> **Lesson:** the transferable output of this benchmark is the *method*, not the leaderboard. Rerun it on your model and your task distribution before you act on the ordering.

## 9. Running this against your own stack

The design is reproducible with modest infrastructure, and it is the concrete form of the eval loop 11.14 asks for:

1. **Pin a golden checkpoint.** One image, restored fresh per trial. Warm state is the bias that will otherwise dominate your results.
2. **Route every harness through one gateway.** Otherwise you measure provider infrastructure alongside the harness.
3. **Use deterministic verifiers.** Reserve LLM judges for tasks with no verifier; a judge shared across configurations still drifts, and drift is indistinguishable from a harness effect.
4. **Report cost per pass, not cost per task.** Cost per task rewards cheap failure; cost per pass prices the retries you will actually pay for.
5. **Log token-weighted cache hit rate and turn count per trial.** These are the two variables §4 and §5 show explain most of the cost spread, and they are the ones you can act on.
6. **Sweep presets, not just vendors.** §6 shows the within-vendor spread rivals the between-vendor spread.
