# 11.14. Tuning the Harness, Not the Model — The Harness as an Optimization Surface

> Companion to [11.1. What Is a Harness](./11.1.%20What%20Is%20a%20Harness.md) and
> [11.11. Operational Concerns](./11.11.%20Operational%20Concerns.md). Those notes
> treat the harness as something you *configure* and *govern*. This note treats it
> as something you *optimize* — a measurable surface whose tuning can close most of
> the gap between an open model and a frontier one, at a fraction of the cost.
> Evidence is drawn from LangChain's July 2026 Nemotron 3 Ultra playbook; the
> lessons generalize to any agent you run over a fixed model.
>
> For the controlled cross-harness measurement this note asks for — one model, one task set,
> twelve harness configurations — see [11.20. Harness Benchmarking — Controlled Cross-Harness Evaluation](./11.20.%20Harness%20Benchmarking%20%E2%80%94%20Controlled%20Cross-Harness%20Evaluation.md).

Most agent teams reach for a bigger model when the agent underperforms. That is
often the wrong lever. A large share of agent failures are **scaffolding
failures** — the model can do the task but the harness makes it fight the
prompt, the tool descriptions, or the control flow. Those failures are fixable
without touching weights, and the fix is cheap. The thesis of this note: the
harness is a first-class optimization surface with its own eval loop, its own
economics, and its own hard ceiling.

---

## 1. The two failure classes

Before tuning anything, decide which of two things is wrong:

| Failure class | What it looks like | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Harness failure** | The model *can* do the step in isolation but does it wrong in the loop: ignores a tool it should call, loops on retries, drops context mid-trajectory, mis-formats output. | Prompts, middleware, tool descriptions — no retraining. |
| **Model failure** | The task is simply beyond the weights: a reasoning step it cannot make, knowledge it does not have. | Post-training, or a different model. |

The trap is spending a training budget on what is actually a harness bug, or
endlessly rewriting prompts against a wall that is really a capability limit.

> **Why it matters:** these two look identical from the outside — "the agent got
> it wrong." Separating them is the whole game. Tune the wrong surface and you
> burn weeks for nothing.

---

## 2. How to tell them apart: per-capability eval and the plateau signal

You cannot separate the two failure classes with a single aggregate score. You
need an eval suite that scores **capabilities separately** — file operations,
tool use, information retrieval, multi-turn conversation, summarization — so a
regression localizes to a specific behavior rather than smearing across one
number. A per-capability breakdown is what turns "the agent is worse" into "the
agent stopped calling the search tool on turn 3."

The signal that tells you to stop tuning the harness:

> **Lesson:** a result that holds steady through everything the harness can reach
> is pointing *past* the harness. When a capability's score plateaus despite
> prompt, middleware, and tool changes, the remaining gap is in the weights — that
> is your cue to escalate to post-training or a stronger model, not to keep
> editing scaffolding.

The plateau is the boundary between §1's two columns, made observable.

---

## 3. What you actually tune

Harness tuning is not "rewrite the system prompt." It is a small set of targeted
surfaces:

| Surface | What it means | Discipline |
|---|---|---|
| **Prompts** | Short, targeted instruction blocks that address a *specific observed failure*, not broad rewrites. | One failure → one block. Broad rewrites regress things you weren't measuring. |
| **Middleware — enforcement** | Call caps, retry logic, timeouts, output validators wrapped around the model's actions. | Constrains pathological loops the model won't self-correct. |
| **Middleware — context engineering** | Injecting guidance at the exact trajectory point where the model fails, not globally. | See [11.8. Context Engineering in the Harness](./11.8.%20Context%20Engineering%20in%20the%20Harness.md). Right context, right step. |
| **Tool descriptions** | Reshaping how tools present themselves — often done *through* middleware rather than editing the tool. | The model's tool choice is only as good as the description it reads. |

Note what is absent: no weight updates, no fine-tuning dataset, no RLHF. Every
change is to the layer *around* the model. This is exactly the surface catalog of
[11.1](./11.1.%20What%20Is%20a%20Harness.md) — now treated as tuning knobs.

---

## 4. The tuning loop

Harness tuning without discipline degenerates into vibes-driven prompt
fiddling. The loop that keeps it honest:

```text
  observe failure in a trace
        │
        ▼
  propose one change (prompt / middleware / tool desc)
        │
        ▼
  SCREEN: run on a small representative sample   ──► no signal? discard
        │
        ▼
  REPEAT: survive multiple trials (not one lucky run)
        │
        ▼
  REGRESSION CHECK: did any other capability drop?  ──► regressed? discard
        │
        ▼
  FULL SUITE: validate on the complete eval
        │
        ▼
  keep it
```

Four properties make this trustworthy, and each maps to a common way ad-hoc
tuning lies to you:

- **Trace-driven.** Every change starts from an *observed* failure in a real
  trajectory, not a guess about what might help.
- **Screened cheaply first.** Candidate changes run on a small sample before they
  are allowed near the full (expensive) suite. Most changes die here.
- **Repetition-gated.** A win must repeat across trials. Agent evals are noisy; a
  single good run is not evidence.
- **Regression-checked.** A change earns its place only if it improves the target
  capability *and regresses nothing else*. This is the guardrail against the
  prompt that fixes retrieval but quietly breaks summarization.

> **Architectural takeaway:** this is the same rigor you would apply to a model
> eval ([6. Evaluation & Observability](../6.%20Evaluation%20%26%20Observability/README.md)),
> pointed at the harness instead. The object under test changed; the method did
> not. [11.11 §3–4](./11.11.%20Operational%20Concerns.md) covers the drift and eval
> plumbing this loop rides on.

An advanced version closes the loop with agents that *propose and verify* harness
changes automatically — an eval-in-the-loop optimizer over the scaffolding. The
discipline above is what keeps that automation from overfitting to noise.

## 5. The economics

The reason this matters is the cost curve. LangChain's published run on the Deep
Agents suite, tuning the harness for an open model against a frontier baseline:

| Configuration | Score (typical / best) | Cost per full suite | Latency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Open model, **no harness profile** | 0.80 baseline | ~$4.48 | ~10 s/test |
| Open model, **tuned harness** | 0.84 / 0.86 | ~$4.48 | ~10 s/test |
| Frontier model, out of the box | 0.86 / 0.87 | ~$43.48 | ~10 s/test |

Harness tuning moved the open model from 0.80 to a best of 0.86 — within one
point of the frontier model's 0.87 — at **roughly one-tenth the cost per run**
and latency at parity. The tuning added no inference cost of its own: it is a
one-time engineering investment amortized across every future run.

> **Why it matters:** the harness-only gain (≈6 points) exceeds the 2–4 point
> improvements routinely reported as meaningful *model* advances. For a large
> class of agent workloads, the harness — not the model — is the dominant lever on
> both quality and spend. A 10x cost gap is not a rounding error; it is the
> difference between a feature that ships and one that doesn't.

This reframes the token-economics argument from
[Multi-Agent Orchestration in Production](../5.%20AI%20Agents%20%26%20Tool%20Use/Multi-Agent%20Orchestration%20in%20Production%20%E2%80%94%20Topologies%2C%20Token%20Economics%2C%20and%20Coordination%20Failure.md):
before you pay frontier-model rates on every call, check whether a tuned harness
on a cheaper model clears your bar.

---

## 6. The ceiling

Harness tuning is not magic, and pretending otherwise wastes budget the other
direction.

> **Lesson:** the harness can fix how the model *behaves*; it cannot add what
> isn't in the model. Scaffolding removes friction between the model's capability
> and the task. It cannot manufacture capability the weights don't hold.

Concretely, the ceiling shows up as §2's plateau. Once a capability stops
responding to prompt, middleware, and tool changes, you have extracted what the
harness can give. The remaining gap is a *model* gap, and the correct move
switches lever: post-train, or upgrade. Recognizing the plateau early is as
valuable as the tuning itself — it stops you from grinding scaffolding against a
capability wall.

---

## 7. What it means for builders

| You are… | Do this |
|---|---|
| **Underperforming agent, reaching for a bigger model** | First run a per-capability eval. If failures localize to behavior (tool choice, looping, formatting), tune the harness — it's ~10x cheaper than the model upgrade and often closes most of the gap. |
| **Choosing between an open and a frontier model** | Don't compare them bare. Compare a *tuned harness on the open model* against the frontier model out of the box. The gap is frequently within a point, at a tenth of the cost. |
| **Running the tuning loop** | Trace-driven, screened cheap, repetition-gated, regression-checked. One failure → one targeted change. Never trust a single good run. |
| **Hitting a plateau** | Stop tuning. A score that won't move against the harness is a weights problem. Escalate to post-training or a stronger model. |

The harness is not just something you configure once and govern. It is a surface
you *optimize continuously*, with measurable leverage over both quality and cost
that frequently beats the model-upgrade lever most teams reach for first.
