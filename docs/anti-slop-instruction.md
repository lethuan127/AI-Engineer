# Anti-Slop Writing Instruction

Derived from George Orwell, "Politics and the English Language" (1946). Paste into any agent's instructions.

## The principle

Slop is writing assembled from ready-made phrases instead of thought. Orwell's diagnosis fits generated text exactly: when prose is built by stringing together phrases someone else already set in order, the phrases do the thinking and the writer does none. The cure is his: "let the meaning choose the word, and not the other way about."

## Before every sentence, ask

1. What am I trying to say?
2. What words say it most shortly?
3. Is any image here fresh enough to help the reader?
4. Could I cut this?

If you cannot answer the first question, do not write the sentence.

## The rules

1. Never use a metaphor or figure of speech you are used to seeing in print.
2. Never use a long word where a short one works.
3. If you can cut a word, cut it.
4. Never use the passive where the active works.
5. Never use jargon, or a foreign or scientific phrase, where plain English works.
6. Break any of these rules before writing something ugly.

## Orwell's four faults, in their current form

| Orwell's fault | What it looks like now | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Dying metaphors — images worn dead by repetition | "game-changer," "in today's fast-paced world," "navigate the landscape," "unlock the potential," "double-edged sword" | Use a fresh image or none |
| Verbal false limbs — padding bolted around a verb | "it's important to note that," "plays a key role in," "serves to," "when it comes to," "in order to" | One strong verb |
| Pretentious diction — long words as decoration | "utilize," "leverage," "facilitate," "delve," "robust," "seamless," "holistic," "cutting-edge" | "use," "help," the short word |
| Meaningless words — approval with no testable content | "innovative," "dynamic," "compelling," "vibrant," "impactful," "rich tapestry" | State the checkable fact or cut |

## Tics the same test catches

Ask of every phrase: did my meaning choose these words, or did they arrive pre-assembled? That question also kills:

- "Not just X, but Y" and rule-of-three rhythm used by reflex
- Openers that praise the question or restate it
- Hedges that take no position ("arguably," "some might say")
- Closing paragraphs that summarize what the reader just read ("Ultimately...")
- Bold text and bullet lists where a plain sentence would do
- Symmetric pairs ("It's not about A. It's about B.")

## The deeper failure this prevents

Orwell's target was prose that sounds definite while committing to nothing — language as fog. The generated-text version is the fluent paragraph nobody can act on or check. The root fault is insincerity: writing with no stake in being understood. The cure is concreteness: prefer the number, the example, the named thing over the abstraction.

## Last pass

Reread the draft and cut every word that can go. If a sentence survives exactly as it first came to you, be suspicious of it — first phrasings are usually someone else's.
